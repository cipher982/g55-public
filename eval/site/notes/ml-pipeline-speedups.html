What was asked

Across 142 recorded GPU hours of closed-loop evaluation and model training on a workstation with two 230 W 24 GB GPUs, 64 CPU cores across 4 NUMA nodes, and 251 GiB of RAM, hardware counters showed the closed-loop simulator keeping a GPU busy 28 % of the time while spending 152 ms per rendered view in CPU image warps and OpenCV Telea hole inpainting. Vision-adapter training ran single-sample float32 loops at 6.2 to 11.3 samples/s after spending 19 to 21 s converting the ONNX graph on every launch, random-access drive evaluation decoded 34 video frames and ran 33 cold warm-up steps per timestamp (1.39 s per jump), and jobs ran on a single GPU without NUMA socket pinning. The question was how much wall-clock time comes out when those CPU, precision, I/O and single-GPU bottlenecks are removed one slice at a time, and where numerical or trajectory behavior changes when exact CPU operations are replaced by fast GPU approximations.

What was done

  • Cached graph load, batched vmap, and bfloat16 backbone with float32 adapter weights. Converted PyTorch model graphs are keyed by ONNX SHA-256, code hash, and precision, then reloaded via memory mapping in a user-private cache. Frozen backbone weights and internal cast nodes run in bfloat16 under torch.vmap inside a scoped precision context while low-rank adapter parameters, low-rank weight accumulation, and per-sample masked losses stay in float32. Merged ONNX exports fold the learned delta onto the original float16 ONNX initializers (decoded in float32) so untouched weights retain every mantissa bit.
  • All-GPU closed-loop rollout renderer (cuda_fast). Batched float32 depth-ordered point splatting, GPU 3x3 morphological closing, a 5-level pull-push pyramid hole fill, precomputed grid_sample dual-camera perspective warps, and fixed-point BT.601 CUDA BGR -> NV12 conversion replace the 16-thread CPU OpenCV path inside the batched sweep and RL environment, while keeping the CPU OpenCV Telea path (default) unchanged for reference evaluations.
  • Memory-mapped 5 Hz drive packs. Warped dual-camera YUV inputs, [32, 512] float16 spatial vision tokens, [16384] vision hidden vectors, and pre-rolled 5 Hz recurrent feature queues are built once per drive (149.2 s for a 2,821-step drive, including 123.4 s video decode + ORT state roll and 24.7 s spatial-token extraction) and opened via mmap in 3.4 ms so random-access evaluation and perception-head training skip video decoding and 33-step cold warm-up.
  • NUMA-pinned dual-GPU sharding and NVLink data-parallel training. Each GPU process verifies PCI bus topology and pins CPU affinity to its local NUMA socket, shards long drives in 4,096-frame chunks with a 1-frame previous-image overlap, shards closed-loop rollout windows across both GPUs, and synchronizes all 226 float32 low-rank adapter tensors (12.6 MiB) over 4-lane NVLink via NCCL.

In the single-drive benchmark before optimization, NVML showed the vision-refresh pass already at 90 % GPU utilization (229 W) at 92.7 frames/s while the unbatched training epoch sat at 46 % mean / 60 % median GPU utilization (184 W) at 11.3 samples/s; batching with vmap in bfloat16 moved refresh to 377.6 frames/s at 84 % GPU utilization (226 W) and cut training epoch time from 75.2 s to 11.5 s (230 W peak before CPU ONNX serialization). In 5 Hz closed-loop rollouts, GPU utilization moved from 29 % mean / 0 % median (183 W) under default to 76 % mean / 84 % median (223 W) under cuda_fast. Scored on the identical 60,787 steps and 4,552 strict paint frames across all 10 validation drives, the 1.26-hour bfloat16 adapter matched the 3.41-hour float32 adapter within 1.6 mm on lane paint (0.030 / 0.025 m vs 0.030 / 0.026 m left / right MAE, 0.04 % vs 0.04 % wheel on line, 0.184 m vs 0.177 m front-vehicle lateral MAE; 904 vs 896 subnormal weights out of 348.9M flushed to zero on float16 ONNX export).

In the 60-episode closed-loop comparison between cuda_fast and default, aggregate pushed relative recovery (0.2083 vs 0.2083), nominal lane MAE (0.3126 m vs 0.3126 m), and binary recovery flips (0) matched, while the trajectory-equivalence test flagged earlier path separation than a single-step 1e-3 steering perturbation (median / p90 / max trajectory gap 0.24 / 3.09 / 6.35 m vs 0.12 / 0.84 / 2.61 m perturbation floor, onset 2.4 s vs 3.0 s, onset p = 0.00098, 4 vs 1 discordant relative-recovery episodes out of 48) because pull-push pyramid hole filling and bilinear grid sampling differ at the pixel level from CPU OpenCV Telea inpainting. Between 1-GPU (batches of 48 + 12) and 2-GPU (batches of 30 + 30) cuda_fast rollouts, batch-size kernel selection produced 0.0087 mean / 0.384 max action difference across 2,160 steps, 0.825 m max episode lane-MAE difference, and 1 discordant relative-recovery episode out of 48.

What it does not show

  • Trajectory-level equivalence between cuda_fast and the CPU OpenCV Telea renderer: cuda_fast is an approximate high-throughput rollout mode for RL, DAgger and broad sweeps, whereas default remains the promotion gate and reference simulator.
  • One-time preprocessing amortization on single-pass drive inspection: building a 5 Hz drive pack takes 149.2 s on a 2,821-step drive before subsequent random-access opens drop to 3.4 ms.
  • Any change to the truck's in-car runtime latency on the Jetson (which executes TensorRT engines) or to the published comma-model ranking table (which continues to run on the unchanged CPU reference simulator).
