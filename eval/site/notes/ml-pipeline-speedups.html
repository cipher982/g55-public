What was asked

Across 142 recorded GPU hours of closed-loop evaluation and model training on a workstation with two 230 W 24 GB GPUs, 64 CPU cores and 251 GiB of RAM, hardware counters showed the closed-loop simulator keeping a GPU busy 28 % of the time while spending 152 ms per rendered view in CPU image warps and OpenCV Telea hole inpainting. Vision-adapter training ran single-sample float32 loops at 6.2 to 11.3 samples/s after spending 20 s converting the ONNX graph on every launch, random-access drive evaluation decoded 34 video frames and ran 33 cold warm-up steps per timestamp (1.39 s per jump), and workloads ran on one GPU without NUMA socket pinning. The question was how much wall-clock time comes out when those CPU, precision, I/O and single-GPU bottlenecks are removed one slice at a time while checking numerical and closed-loop parity after each step.

What was done

  • Cached graph load, batched vmap, and bfloat16 backbone with float32 adapter weights. Converted PyTorch model graphs are keyed by ONNX SHA-256 and reloaded with memory mapping. Frozen backbone weights and internal cast nodes run in bfloat16 under torch.vmap while low-rank adapter parameters and per-sample masked losses stay in float32, and merged ONNX exports fold the learned delta directly onto the original float32 initializers so untouched weights keep every mantissa bit.
  • All-GPU closed-loop environment renderer (cuda_fast). Batched float32 depth-ordered point splatting, GPU 3x3 morphological closing, a 5-level pull-push pyramid hole fill, precomputed grid_sample dual-camera perspective warps, and fixed-point BT.601 CUDA BGR -> NV12 conversion replace the 16-thread CPU OpenCV path inside the batched training and sweep environment, while leaving the single-episode CPU reference simulator unchanged.
  • Memory-mapped 5 Hz drive packs. Warped dual-camera YUV inputs, [32, 512] float16 spatial vision tokens, [16384] vision hidden vectors, and pre-rolled 5 Hz recurrent feature queues are written once per drive and opened via mmap in 3.4 ms so random-access evaluation and perception-head training skip video decoding and 33-step cold warm-up.
  • NUMA-pinned dual-GPU sharding and NVLink data-parallel training. Each GPU process pins CPU affinity to its local PCIe root complex NUMA node, shards long drives in 4,096-frame chunks with a 1-frame previous-image overlap for bit-exact two-frame vision inputs, shards closed-loop rollout windows across both GPUs, and synchronizes float32 low-rank adapter gradients over 4-lane NVLink via NCCL.

During pure vision-refresh execution on one GPU, median NVML GPU utilization moved from 46 % (184 W) in the unbatched loop to 84 to 100 % at 226 to 228 W against the 230 W cap; in closed-loop 5 Hz rollouts, median GPU utilization moved from 0 % (29 % mean, 183 W) to 84 % (76 % mean, 223 W). Scored end-to-end on the 10 unseen validation drives after 4 full epochs in bfloat16 and exported to standard float16 ONNX, left / right paint error was 0.030 / 0.024 m (against 0.049 / 0.036 m for the 3.01-hour float32 run), wheel encroachment stayed at 0.0 %, speed over CAN was 1.006, and front-vehicle lateral MAE was 0.184 m (against 0.187 m).

What it does not show

  • Any change to the truck's in-car runtime latency on the Jetson, which runs TensorRT engines rather than PyTorch training graphs or simulator reprojection.
  • Any change to the published comma-model table scores, which continue to run through the unchanged CPU reference simulator so historical numbers remain byte-reproducible.
