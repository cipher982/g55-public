What was asked

comma's driving policy is a base policy plus a learned correction (the residual head). Can the correction alone be trained, in closed loop in the simulator on the truck's drives, by imitating a teacher, so that the truck returns to its line sooner after a disturbance without driving worse when nothing disturbs it? This note was first published as a candidate result. Three outside reviews and a control run later showed that the comparison behind it used the wrong baseline, so it is rewritten with the corrected result first.

What was done

Every run trained only the correction head, starting from the truck's adapted model, on 73 windows from four recorded drives of one truck and one driver, and was scored on a 30-window validation panel (6 other drives), paired by window, 95% intervals over windows. The closed-loop push bench steers a simulated vehicle while the camera view is redrawn from recorded frames. Two push types were used, 120 episodes each: a sideways step of 1.0 or 1.5 m to each side with no change of heading (the equal-offset step), and a push that also turns the truck (the yaw push). Distances are measured from each model's own unpushed run of the same window.

Test one. The teacher steered toward the driver's recorded path, using the true pose from the simulator. Correction at its shipped scale, 8 epochs, one seed.

Test two. The teacher was the policy's own action plus feedback on the truck's deviation from the policy's own unpushed run (an offset gain and a heading gain). Every retrained head started from a version of the correction with its bound doubled (output limited to 2 instead of 1), and was compared with the shipped model, whose bound is 1. Runs: full teacher gains (three seeds), half gains (two seeds), quarter gains, and zero gains (no feedback, so the target is the start policy's own action).

Controls. The teacher's feedback was run as the controller on the shipped model with no learning, at quarter, half and full gains. Commanded jerk (the step-to-step change of the lateral acceleration command) was read from every trace. Last, the bound-2 start was exported with zero training updates and scored the same way. That last control is the one that matters.

Result

The comparison had the wrong baseline. The bound-2 start with zero training already gives most of the measured effect on the sideways step. Distance at 4 s against shipped is -0.133 m [-0.174, -0.094], established by 2 s and flat after. Its command is smaller and smoother than shipped (jerk rms 0.71 against 0.92 m/s3 on the step), and ordinary driving does not change (unpushed lane error in turns -0.001 m [-0.013, +0.009]). The yaw push shows the same lead. A zero-gain trained head gives the same step lead, -0.133 m.

Reviewers attribute the lead to the bench's sideways step. Right after the step, the frozen base policy turns the wrong way for about a second on most episodes, and a correction with twice the bound cancels more of that. The lead is a head start made by the bench, not a faster return. This mechanism is the reviewers' reading; the control shows the effect is there with no training, and the mechanism itself was not isolated.

  • At quarter and half gain the teacher adds 8 to 12 cm at 4 s on the step and 15 to 19 cm on the yaw push, with intervals that exclude zero. At full gain it adds 12 cm on the step and nothing distinguishable on the yaw push.
  • The bench's own verdicts against the untrained start are flat: step recovered 63.3, 58.3 and 59.2% against 60.0%; yaw recovered 54.2, 51.7 and 46.7% against 51.7%; yaw departures 7.5% against 9.2%.
  • The extra distance comes with an overshoot on the yaw push (below) and, in three of four runs below full gain, about 10 cm of extra width in ordinary turns. The turn cost belongs to the training: the untrained start has none, and the zero-gain head, with no teacher feedback, is 13 cm wide.
  • The zero-gain head also has a lower unpushed jerk (0.21 against 0.30) and a small non-zero imitation loss at zero gain. Both come from the training, and the cause of the loss was not traced.

Controls that led here. Each is a result in its own right.

  • Gain sweep. Across quarter, half (two seeds) and full gains the step lead at 4 s moves only from -0.213 to -0.254 m, while the lead at 2 s doubles (-0.240 to -0.460 m). A feedback term scaled by four should not leave the outcome that flat. The untrained start explains why: most of the lead was never from the feedback.
  • The teacher run as the controller, no learning. The feedback, read from the simulator's true pose and added to the shipped model's request, overshoots its own line by 0.24, 0.52 and 0.71 m at 3 s on the yaw push at quarter, half and full gains. The trained heads overshoot by the same or less (0.23, 0.35 to 0.38 and 0.49 m). The overshoot is the teacher's, so removing it means changing the teacher. With the offset gain alone (heading gain 0), the step oscillates and the yaw push is worse than shipped (recovered 36.7%), so the heading term is needed.
  • Zero-gain training run. It reproduces the untrained start's step lead and carries the turn cost, which is what exposed the baseline problem.
  • Smoothness. By commanded jerk, full gain is harsher than shipped (about twice on the yaw push: 2.64 against 1.31 m/s3 rms), half gain is about equal, and quarter and zero gain are smoother than shipped. The teacher run as the controller is harsher than the head trained from it at every gain. This is the command, not what the simulated vehicle delivers, and there are no intervals.

Withdrawn. Earlier versions of this note said the step lead "survives a no-training comparator" and that "the trained head recovers like the privileged controller". Both are withdrawn. The first used the lateral delay request as the comparator, and the comparator that mattered was the model's own untrained bound-2 start. The second compared a head on the bound-2 start with a teacher on the bound-1 start, and the head's own increment over its start is under half of the teacher's. "Quarter gain returns sooner and more smoothly than shipped" stays true, and is mostly the bound.

Test one was negative. Re-scored against the model's own unpushed run: distance at 2 s +0.105 m [+0.076, +0.135] (worse), at 4 s -0.025 m [-0.114, +0.056], at 5.6 s +0.056 m [-0.065, +0.182]. Share back within 0.3 m at 5.6 s fell from 70.8% to 57.5%. Unpushed lane error rose from 0.225 to 0.279 m. Outside review found that configuration weak: the judge used a path the trained model's own line no longer sat on, and the teacher's target is a driver bias the policy cannot see in the image.

The yaw-push overshoot. Signed along the push direction, mean offset from each model's own unpushed run at full gain is about -0.5 m at 3 s (shipped +0.27 m), so the trained models cross their line by about 2 s and sit half a metre past it. At half gain it is -0.35 m, at quarter gain -0.23 m.

What it does not show

  • Standing. The branch is parked and is not a method. The bar was that the bench's verdicts move against the right baseline, and they do not. The teacher's increment costs overshoot and, in turns, width.
  • What would reopen it: a push that is not a teleport of the vehicle; a teacher without the overshoot; scoring every model against one common reference line, with jerk measured on what the vehicle delivers instead of what is commanded; and a panel that has not been searched repeatedly.
  • The measure. Speed of return alone rewards jerking the wheel. The question is return for a given smoothness. The distance-from-own-line measure used here also flatters a head whose own line moved, and the commanded jerk numbers are a first look.
  • The panel. One small panel of 30 windows, reused many times, and the gains in the teacher sweep could overfit it. The split between training and validation is by drive only; one validation drive continues a training drive. Seeds vary the optimiser only.
  • The raised bound elsewhere. The finding that raising the bound alone returns no faster from an equal offset came from a different design, where the bound was raised only partway through a yaw push. Applied from step 0, the bound-2 start is 0.13 m closer at 4 s on this panel. That is the bench's step, per the reviewers, and the other findings in the raised-bound note are unchanged.
  • Everything is simulation. The simulated vehicle has no tyre or comfort limit, and a peak command above 3 m/s2 was not tested on a real vehicle. Whether the heads read the image or the model's recurrent state was not tested.
  • Models exported after the trainer's stop alarm are test articles. Nothing is deployed.
