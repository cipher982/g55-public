What was asked

comma's driving policy is a base policy plus a learned correction (the residual head). Can the correction alone be trained, in closed loop in the simulator on the truck's drives, by imitating a teacher, so that the truck returns to its line sooner after a disturbance without driving worse when nothing disturbs it? Two quick tests were run on the same day, one after the other. The second was designed from the reviews of the first, and later runs and a second round of outside review (two outside reviews in all) changed how it reads. This note uses the corrected reading.

What was done

Both tests trained only the correction head, starting from the truck's adapted model, on 73 windows from four recorded drives of one truck and one driver. Both were scored on the 30-window validation panel (6 other drives), paired by window against the shipped adapter, 95% intervals over windows. The closed-loop push bench steers a simulated vehicle while the camera view is redrawn from recorded frames; a push moves the truck sideways by 1.0 or 1.5 m, to each side, which gives 120 pushed episodes. One seed in test one, three in test two, plus controls and a half-gain run described below.

Test one. The teacher steered toward the driver's recorded path, using the true pose from the simulator. 8 epochs, correction at its shipped scale.

Test two. The teacher was the policy's own action plus feedback on the truck's deviation from the policy's own unpushed run of the same window at the same step, so the unpushed line cannot move by construction. The correction started at twice its shipped scale, with the smoothness costs off and low exploration noise, 10 epochs. Each of three seeds was stopped by the trainer's own cut alarm at the last epoch and exported by hand. Besides the yaw push, an equal-offset control was run: a sideways step with no yaw, so there is nothing to resist and both models start their return from the same place. The best setting that needs no training (a lateral delay request of 0.775 s, see the lateral delay request note) was run on the same step as a comparator. Three later controls on the same panel are described under Result: a cap on the raw lateral request, a cut to steps where every model's rendered view is valid, and the same recipe at half the teacher's gains.

Result

Test one was negative. The first headline (recovered share against the driver's path fell from 0.492 to 0.333) mixed a moved unpushed line with failed recovery. Re-scored against the model's own unpushed run: distance at 2 s +0.105 m [+0.076, +0.135] (worse by about 0.1 m), at 4 s -0.025 m [-0.114, +0.056], at 5.6 s +0.056 m [-0.065, +0.182]. Share back within 0.3 m at 4 s 42.5% to 40.8%; at 5.6 s 70.8% to 57.5% (-13.3 points [-23.3, -4.2]). Unpushed lane error rose from 0.225 to 0.279 m. Outside review found the test weak for these reasons: the judge compared against a path the trained model's own line no longer sat on; a teacher that steers to the driver's path from the true pose teaches the policy an average bias, because the policy sees the lane and not the driver's line; the recipe was not given a chance (correction at its shipped scale, where the output cannot reach the teacher's demands; smoothness costs still half of the loss; 45 to 58% of pushed training episodes cut short; at most 31 of the 73 windows ever drawn). The result says this configuration did not help. It does not say training the correction fails.

One reviewer's explanation, untested: the teacher's heading term commands up to 1.7 to 2.55 m/s2 against the first half of the push, and the second half then turns the truck the way the model is already steering.

The 4 s lead stays under both caps (-0.224 and -0.214 m against -0.254 uncapped), so the lead does not come from peak command. It comes from holding a larger command for longer. The cap itself harms ordinary driving (unpushed lane error 0.236 m uncapped, 0.346 m at 1.5, 0.428 m at 1.2, because turns on this panel ask for more than the cap), so the capped rows are a control, not a setting. Validity cut. Cut to steps where every model's rendered view has been valid so far (110 to 119 of 120 episodes), the 4 s lead on the step is -0.219 m [-0.286, -0.153] (2 s: -0.456 [-0.494, -0.420]). The step lead is not made by invalid views. The bench's own recovered shares on the step do count invalid views differently per model, so part of its gain there is fewer invalid views.

About 90% of the 4 s step lead stays. Ordinary-driving command activity rises 21% in place of 39 to 67%. The yaw-push verdicts are no longer worse than shipped (recovered 51.7% against 49.2%, departures 7.5% against 15.8%). The overshoot is smaller and still present (-0.35 m at 3 s against -0.49 at full gain). Lane error in ordinary driving is 10 cm higher on the 9 turn windows; the full-gain seeds range from +2 to +10 cm on the same windows, so one seed cannot separate the two. The step lead and the overshoot did not scale together, but nothing here shows the overshoot can be removed.

The trainer's own evaluation on training windows also got worse in every run (the stopping alarm above); with valid views only, the first run's deterioration was all newly invalid renders at the end of the push, where hard counter-steer takes marginal views past the coverage limit.

What it does not show

  • It is a candidate, not a proven improvement. The step lead replicates across three seeds on one disturbance type (a sideways offset with no yaw) and survives the command cap and the validity cut. Open against it: the yaw-push overshoot (smaller at half gain, still present); the higher command (about twice the sustained command on the step); the cost in turns, which one seed cannot separate from the seed spread; the score being the training target (teacher and measure are both distance from the policy's own unpushed run, so the test is whether the head can read that offset from the image on new road); one small reused panel; and optimiser seeds only (same training windows, same panel). A second half-gain seed and a quarter-gain run are in progress.
  • Everything is simulation. The simulated vehicle has no tyre or comfort limit, which makes a fast return cheap, and a peak command above 3 m/s2 is not tested on a real vehicle.
  • One small panel of 30 windows, reused many times that day. The split between training and validation is by drive only; one validation drive is the continuation of a training drive. The results hold without it, but the split is not clean.
  • The first reading of test two was overstated, twice. It first reported the truck displaced about half as far during a push; the first outside review corrected that (being displaced less during a push is not the same as returning better; a comparison at 2 s alone was the one time that favoured the trained model), and the equal-offset control was run because of it. That control was then described as a return about twice as fast. The second outside review and a signed re-score corrected this: it is an earlier, harder turn-back at about twice the command, and the later behaviour after the yaw push is an overshoot past the model's own line, not a drift.
  • Not tried: whether the head uses the image during a push (it is not a constant gain on the command when nothing pushes it), a clean split with a larger panel, and a quarter-gain run (in progress). The cause of the overshoot is not established; the teacher's heading term is a guess, untested.
  • Models exported after the trainer's stop alarm are test articles, not candidates for the truck. Nothing is deployed.
