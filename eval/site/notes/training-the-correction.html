What was asked

comma's driving policy is a base policy plus a learned correction (the residual head). Can the correction alone be trained, in closed loop in the simulator on the truck's drives, by imitating a teacher, so that the truck returns to its line sooner after a disturbance without driving worse when nothing disturbs it? This note was first published as a candidate result. Three outside reviews and a control run later showed that the comparison behind it used the wrong baseline, so it is rewritten with the corrected result first. A later run on a push with no sideways jump (section "Update, 2026-11-16") gives the teacher a clearer effect, which a fourth review reads as smaller displacement while the push is running, not a faster return. A planned test on 38 windows from 11 new drives then found the same effect and closed the work; see the last update.

What was done

Every run trained only the correction head, starting from the truck's adapted model, on 73 windows from four recorded drives of one truck and one driver, and was scored on a 30-window validation panel (6 other drives), paired by window, 95% intervals over windows. The closed-loop push bench steers a simulated vehicle while the camera view is redrawn from recorded frames. Two push types were used, 120 episodes each: a sideways step of 1.0 or 1.5 m to each side with no change of heading (the equal-offset step), and a push that also turns the truck (the yaw push). Distances are measured from each model's own unpushed run of the same window.

Test one. The teacher steered toward the driver's recorded path, using the true pose from the simulator. Correction at its shipped scale, 8 epochs, one seed.

Test two. The teacher was the policy's own action plus feedback on the truck's deviation from the policy's own unpushed run (an offset gain and a heading gain). Every retrained head started from a version of the correction with its bound doubled (output limited to 2 instead of 1), and was compared with the shipped model, whose bound is 1. Runs: full teacher gains (three seeds), half gains (two seeds), quarter gains, and zero gains (no feedback, so the target is the start policy's own action).

Controls. The teacher's feedback was run as the controller on the shipped model with no learning, at quarter, half and full gains. Commanded jerk (the step-to-step change of the lateral acceleration command) was read from every trace. Last, the bound-2 start was exported with zero training updates and scored the same way. That last control is the one that matters.

Result

The comparison had the wrong baseline. The bound-2 start with zero training already gives most of the measured effect on the sideways step. Distance at 4 s against shipped is -0.133 m [-0.174, -0.094], established by 2 s and flat after. Its command is smaller and smoother than shipped (jerk rms 0.71 against 0.92 m/s3 on the step), and ordinary driving does not change (unpushed lane error in turns -0.001 m [-0.013, +0.009]). The yaw push shows the same lead. A zero-gain trained head gives the same step lead, -0.133 m.

Reviewers attribute the lead to the bench's sideways step. Right after the step, the frozen base policy turns the wrong way for about a second on most episodes, and a correction with twice the bound cancels more of that. The lead is a head start made by the bench, not a faster return. This mechanism is the reviewers' reading; the control shows the effect is there with no training, and the mechanism itself was not isolated.

Controls that led here. Each is a result in its own right.

  • Gain sweep. Across quarter, half (two seeds) and full gains the step lead at 4 s moves only from -0.213 to -0.254 m, while the lead at 2 s doubles (-0.240 to -0.460 m). A feedback term scaled by four should not leave the outcome that flat. The untrained start explains why: most of the lead was never from the feedback.
  • The teacher run as the controller, no learning. The feedback, read from the simulator's true pose and added to the shipped model's request, overshoots its own line by 0.24, 0.52 and 0.71 m at 3 s on the yaw push at quarter, half and full gains. The trained heads overshoot by the same or less (0.23, 0.35 to 0.38 and 0.49 m). The overshoot is the teacher's, so removing it means changing the teacher. With the offset gain alone (heading gain 0), the step oscillates and the yaw push is worse than shipped (recovered 36.7%), so the heading term is needed.
  • Zero-gain training run. It reproduces the untrained start's step lead and carries the turn cost, which is what exposed the baseline problem.
  • Smoothness. By commanded jerk, full gain is harsher than shipped (about twice on the yaw push: 2.64 against 1.31 m/s3 rms), half gain is about equal, and quarter and zero gain are smoother than shipped. The teacher run as the controller is harsher than the head trained from it at every gain. This is the command, not what the simulated vehicle delivers, and there are no intervals.

Withdrawn. Earlier versions of this note said the step lead "survives a no-training comparator" and that "the trained head recovers like the privileged controller". Both are withdrawn. The first used the lateral delay request as the comparator, and the comparator that mattered was the model's own untrained bound-2 start. The second compared a head on the bound-2 start with a teacher on the bound-1 start, and the head's own increment over its start is under half of the teacher's. "Quarter gain returns sooner and more smoothly than shipped" stays true, and is mostly the bound.

Test one was negative. Re-scored against the model's own unpushed run: distance at 2 s +0.105 m [+0.076, +0.135] (worse), at 4 s -0.025 m [-0.114, +0.056], at 5.6 s +0.056 m [-0.065, +0.182]. Share back within 0.3 m at 5.6 s fell from 70.8% to 57.5%. Unpushed lane error rose from 0.225 to 0.279 m. Outside review found that configuration weak: the judge used a path the trained model's own line no longer sat on, and the teacher's target is a driver bias the policy cannot see in the image.

The yaw-push overshoot. Signed along the push direction, mean offset from each model's own unpushed run at full gain is about -0.5 m at 3 s (shipped +0.27 m), so the trained models cross their line by about 2 s and sit half a metre past it. At half gain it is -0.35 m, at quarter gain -0.23 m.

Update, 2026-11-16: a push with no sideways jump

The third review attributed the untrained start's lead to the step being a jump of the vehicle. To test that, the bench's slow-drift push was run once on seven fixed models: shipped, the untrained doubled-limit start, and trained heads at zero, quarter, half (two seeds) and full teacher strength. The push is the yaw push stretched over 16 steps (3.2 s) with no jump. No model saw it in training. How to read it. A fourth outside review read the bench code and found that this is smaller displacement while the push is running, not a faster return. The push steers against the model for all 3.2 s, and every model is carried through its own line by 4 s (the untrained start is +0.49 m on one side at 2 s and -0.31 m on the other at 4 s), so the 4 s and 5.6 s differences measure a smaller swing. This note adopts that reading. Four further points from the review:

  • Lane contact is the largest distance from the recorded path in the first 4 s being over 0.87 m. The push fills 3.2 s of that window, so the fall in contact restates the smaller excursion and carries no information about recovery.
  • The bench's recovered verdict cannot register this effect. Unpushed runs meet its condition only 60 to 67% of the time, and the pushed runs (48 to 58%) sit at that ceiling.
  • The bench treats every push as ending at 2 s (10 steps). On a 16-step push, its post-push measures (pushed lane error, overshoot, the yaw removed from delivered jerk) start while the push is still running. The distance tables above do not use them; the pushed lane error does. The bench is shared and was not changed.
  • Peak commanded jerk at half gain is 32 to 42% above the untrained start, and 69% at full gain. The earlier remark that average jerk does not rise hid this.

The teacher as the controller, no learning. The teacher's feedback, read from the simulator's true pose, was added to the shipped model's request on this push with no training. Difference against shipped at 2, 4 and 5.6 s: quarter gain -0.118, -0.071, -0.175 m; half gain -0.211, -0.141, -0.222 m. The trained heads at the same gain get -0.114, -0.097, -0.151 m (quarter) and -0.183 to -0.198, -0.187 to -0.209, -0.211 to -0.213 m (half, two seeds). From the camera, the head gets 87 to 97% of the teacher's reduction at 2 s and all of it later. Commanded jerk is lower for the head (rms 0.72 and 0.74 against 0.97 at half gain) and the peak is the same (2.80 and 3.00 against 2.99 m/s3). The head copies the feedback law from the image, somewhat more smoothly, and adds nothing beyond it. The technique, injecting disturbances and training toward a stabilising expert that sees the true state, is standard (DART and DAgger style), so no claim of novelty is made.

Update, 2026-11-16: the planned test on new drives, and why the work is closed

The earlier updates left one open question: does the trained correction beat a setting that needs no training, on drives that no choice in this work was made on? The test was written down before it was run. The models were frozen: the shipped model, the untrained doubled-limit start, and two half-gain trained heads (two seeds) that were not touched after training. The panel was 38 windows from 11 drives that this work had never read, picked by a rule applied by script without looking at any model output: drop drives used for training or testing, drop windows with a turn signal on, take up to 4 evenly spaced windows per drive. The push was the slow sideways push (1.0 and 1.5 m, both sides). Distances are measured against the recorded path, so no model is judged against its own line. Smoothness is the lateral jerk the simulated vehicle actually delivers. The comparison settings need no training: the untrained start with the model's lateral delay request (usually 0.275 s) made 0.2 s and 0.5 s longer, which makes it read its own plan further ahead.

  • The effect is real on new drives. Against the same model untrained, the trained heads are about 20 cm lower at the peak of the push and about 12 cm closer to the path afterwards. The peak is lower on 10 and 11 of the 11 drives for the two seeds.
  • A look-ahead setting gets most of it with no training. Reading the unchanged model's plan 0.5 s further ahead gives about 17 cm at the peak and 9 cm afterwards. The trained heads' remaining 3 to 5 cm lead (against a setting 0.7 s longer, which matches the heads' peak jerk) has intervals that include zero, so 11 drives cannot separate it from no difference.
  • The trained heads are rougher under the push. Their delivered jerk during the push is above every setting in the planned run. In ordinary driving they are slightly smoother than the 0.5 s look-ahead setting (rms jerk 0.028 lower), because a long look-ahead roughens ordinary driving.
  • One ordinary window each wanders. In each trained head, one window with no push ends past the bench's contact line (0.87 m from the recorded path). No no-training setting has one.
  • Looking further ahead stops helping. Largest distance during the push is 0.593, 0.592 and 0.586 m at delay requests 0.5 s, 0.7 s and 0.9 s longer than usual, while jerk keeps rising. This is a property of the simulated vehicle, whose actuator delay is a placeholder. The truck has no steering actuator, so it says nothing about the truck.

The three conditions set in advance, and what happened. One was met. (1) Each trained head below the untrained start on both distance measures, with intervals excluding zero: met. (2) Distance in ordinary driving not worse than the untrained start by more than 2 cm at the top of the interval: not met. The means moved 1.0 cm and 0.1 cm, but the interval tops are 3.5 cm and 2.7 cm. (3) Below the no-training line at the heads' own peak jerk: not met on the planned run, because the heads' peak jerk was above every setting tried. Two longer settings were then run, outside the plan, to bracket it. The heads are lower in the means, by 3 to 6 cm, in both seeds and against all three long settings, and no interval excludes zero.

Outside review. Two fresh-context reviews agreed the work should be closed. They raised these points, which this note adopts. The 11 drives cover only 5 days, and 17 of the 38 windows come from one afternoon, so the intervals are optimistic. Peak jerk was a poor single quantity to match on: the 0.7 s setting has the same push result as the 0.5 s one with more jerk on every measure. On jerk under the push the trained heads are rougher than every look-ahead setting run (rms 0.93 and 0.91 against at most 0.805). And the largest single change on this panel is the doubled limit with no training (25 cm against the shipped model), larger than training on top of it (20 cm).

Conclusion. Correction training has a real effect in the bench: it cuts the slow push's peak excursion by about 20 cm against its untrained start, on drives it never saw. It is not an improvement over a one-number setting that needs no training, which gets about 17 cm, and it is rougher under the push. This line of work is closed. The side finding holds for the bench only: a longer lateral delay request improves slow-push rejection up to about 0.5 s longer than usual, at a cost in ordinary-driving jerk.

What it does not show

  • Standing. The branch is closed (see the 2026-11-16 update on the planned test) and is not a method. The bar was that the bench's verdicts move against the right baseline, and they do not. The teacher's increment costs overshoot and, in turns, width, and on the slow push it costs peak commanded jerk. The slow-drift run shows a repeatable reduction in displacement with a known mechanism, no verdict that can register it, and no claim of novelty. The truck has no steering actuator, so nothing lateral can be tried on it.
  • What would reopen it: a no-training comparison at matched peak delivered jerk (for example a plain gain on the untrained model's lateral output, or a longer delay request, against the head); a bench verdict that is not at its ceiling; a disturbance that is not built from a yaw doublet; and drives that were not used for any choice in this branch. The push without a jump, the first item of the earlier list, has now been run.
  • The measure. Speed of return alone rewards jerking the wheel. The question is return for a given smoothness. The distance-from-own-line measure used here also flatters a head whose own line moved, and the commanded jerk numbers are a first look.
  • The panel. One small panel of 30 windows, reused many times, and the gains in the teacher sweep could overfit it. The slow push was run once after the models were fixed, but gains, recipe and bound were chosen on these windows, and the slow push is the training push stretched 1.6 times, not a different disturbance. The split between training and validation is by drive only; one validation drive continues a training drive. Seeds vary the optimiser only.
  • The raised bound elsewhere. The finding that raising the bound alone returns no faster from an equal offset came from a different design, where the bound was raised only partway through a yaw push. Applied from step 0, the bound-2 start is 0.13 m closer at 4 s on this panel. That is the bench's step, per the reviewers. On the slow push with no jump the bound-2 start is only 3 to 4 cm from shipped, which supports that reading, and the other findings in the raised-bound note are unchanged.
  • Everything is simulation. The simulated vehicle has no tyre or comfort limit, and a peak command above 3 m/s2 was not tested on a real vehicle. Whether the heads read the image or the model's recurrent state was not tested.
  • Models exported after the trainer's stop alarm are test articles. Nothing is deployed.
